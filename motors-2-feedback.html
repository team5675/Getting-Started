Feedforward, then feedback

Predict the volts a speed needs, then let the measured speed correct the prediction.

01 See it Follow the idea02 Try it Work out the volts03 Check it Explain what happened

Today’s small goal

Predict the volts that hold a target speed (feedforward), correct what the prediction missed from the measured speed (feedback), and explain the two ways a feedback gain can be wrong. Motor lab 2 does it on your roller.

Before you start: finish Motor lab 1, so you have your own kS and kV. This page is reading and arithmetic only. Allow 40–55 minutes.

1. See it: feedforward is a prediction

In Motors 1 you turned speed into volts: volts = kS + kV × speed. To hold a target speed, use the target in that sentence. That is feedforward: work out the volts before looking at what the roller does.

One detail: friction always pushes against the motion. Spinning backwards, kS has to push backwards too. Java’s Math.signum gives the sign of a number:

CallReturns
Math.signum(25.0)1.0
Math.signum(-25.0)-1.0
Math.signum(0.0)0.0

So the full feedforward is kS × Math.signum(target) + kV × target. With the practice wheel from Motors 1 (kS = 0.5 V, kV = 0.2 V per rps): a target of 25 rps needs 0.5 + 5 = 5.5 V; −25 rps needs −0.5 − 5 = −5.5 V; and a target of 0 asks for 0 V, so a stopped roller is not pushed at all.

2. See it: why a prediction is not enough

Feedforward never looks at the speed. It is like steering with your eyes closed: fine on a road you know, until something changes.

  • Your kS and kV are measurements, so they are a little off.
  • An object in the roller drags on it. That is a load, and the prediction knows nothing about it.
  • Real robots change: batteries sag, bearings wear, belts loosen.

Each of these makes the real speed differ from the target, and feedforward cannot notice.

3. See it: feedback corrects the difference

Feedback looks at the measured speed and adds volts in proportion to how wrong it is:

error = target − measured
volts = feedforward + kP × error

kP is the feedback gain: extra volts for each 1 rps of error. The P is for “proportional”. A worked example with the practice wheel and kP = 0.5 V per rps:

TargetMeasuredErrorFeedforwardkP × errorVolts
30282 (too slow)0.5 + 6 = 6.5+1.07.5
3031−1 (too fast)6.5−0.56.0
303006.506.5

Read the sign carefully. Too slow makes a positive error and adds volts. Too fast makes a negative error and takes volts away. Writing measured − target by mistake turns feedback into “push harder the wrong way”.

4. See it: kP too small, kP too big

Here is the same roller as in the motor labs, holding 20 rps, with a load added at 2 seconds. Only kP changes.

  • kP = 0 is feedforward only. When the load arrives, the speed falls and stays low.
  • A small kP pulls the speed back up, but not all the way. Feedback only adds volts when there is an error, so a small error has to remain to make the extra push. This leftover is called steady-state error. A bigger kP leaves a smaller one.
  • A kP that is too big overshoots. The measured speed is one loop (20 ms) old, so a big kP reacts hard to old news: it pushes past the target, then pulls back too far, again and again. Much too big, and it never settles.

A good kP sits between those two problems. There are more gains, kI and kD, that can remove the leftover error or calm the swinging. Velocity control on team mechanisms mostly uses kS, kV and kP (sometimes kA, for speeding up), so this course uses those three.

5. See it: tune from numbers, not by feel

  1. Feedforward first, with kP = 0. Without a load, the speed should already be close to the target. If it is far off, measure kS and kV again before touching kP.
  2. Add a small kP, add the load, and read two numbers from the log: the speed under load, and the highest speed reached.
  3. Double kP and repeat, until the error under load is small enough for your requirement.
  4. If the speed overshoots or swings, go back to the last good kP.

Write each try as a row in a table. A row of numbers is evidence a teammate can check; “it looked fine” is not.

6. Try it: work out the volts

Use the practice wheel with kS = 0.5 V, kV = 0.2 V per rps and kP = 0.4 V per rps.

  1. Target 20 rps, measured 20 rps.
    Check

    Error 0. Volts = 0.5 + 0.2 × 20 = 4.5 V.

  2. Target 20 rps, measured 18 rps.
    Check

    Error 2. Volts = 4.5 + 0.4 × 2 = 5.3 V.

  3. Target 20 rps, measured 21 rps.
    Check

    Error −1. Volts = 4.5 − 0.4 = 4.1 V.

  4. Target −20 rps, measured −20 rps.
    Check

    Math.signum(−20) is −1, so volts = −0.5 + 0.2 × (−20) = −4.5 V.

  5. With kP = 0.4, a load leaves 1.7 rps of error. Which makes the error smaller, kP = 0.2 or kP = 0.8? What could go wrong with your choice?
    Check

    kP = 0.8 leaves a smaller error, because each rps of error now pushes twice as hard. The risk is overshoot and swinging. Check the log for both before choosing.

Before you move on

Explain in your own words: what does feedforward know, what does feedback know, and why does a small kP leave some error under a load? Name one sign that kP is too big.

Write one prediction for Motor lab 2 in learning-notes.txt: “With kP = 0, a load will drop my roller from 20 rps to about ___ rps.”

Connection to team code

TalonFX settings have a slot of gains called Slot0, with fields named kS, kV and kP. They mean exactly what they mean here: Motor lab 3 copies your numbers into them. The difference is where the math runs. In Motor lab 2 your robot code does it every 20 ms; in Motor lab 3 the TalonFX does it itself, beside the motor.
