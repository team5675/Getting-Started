1. A map needs a starting corner

Before a robot can say where it is, everyone has to agree on one map. FRC robot programs use a map of the field measured in meters, with one corner as the starting point, called the origin.

For this lesson, use a blue-origin example map: stand behind the blue alliance wall and look out at the field. The origin, written (0, 0), is the corner on your right. The +X direction points away from you, toward the red wall. The +Y direction points to your left. The drawing uses an illustrative 16.54 m by 8.07 m rectangle. These are not confirmed 2027 field dimensions; robot code must use the selected season’s official layout.

The robot also needs to know which way it faces. That is its heading. A heading of 0° means the robot’s front points along +X, toward the red wall. Seen from above, angles grow counterclockwise: 90° means the front points along +Y.

Red alliance tooThe map does not change when you play on red. Red robots use the same corner, the same +X and the same angles. Section 6 explains why that matters.

2. A pose is three numbers

Position plus heading is called a pose. On a flat field, a pose is three numbers:

NumberMeaningExample
xHow far along the field from the blue wall, in meters2.0
yHow far across the field from the right-hand side wall (as seen from blue), in meters1.5
headingWhich way the robot’s front faces, as an angle90°

WPILib gives each idea its own class, just like your PracticeCounter was a class:

ClassHolds
Translation2dA point or an arrow on the map: an x and a y.
Rotation2dAn angle. Rotation2d.fromDegrees(90) makes one from degrees.
Pose2dA Translation2d plus a Rotation2d: where the robot is and which way it faces.

3. Add three imports

Where this goes: open src/main/java/frc/robot/Robot.java in your practice project. Add these lines near the top, below the package frc.robot; line, with any other import lines. Skip any that are already there.

Code to use

import edu.wpi.first.math.geometry.Pose2d;
import edu.wpi.first.math.geometry.Rotation2d;
import edu.wpi.first.math.geometry.Translation2d;

These classes are part of WPILib’s math library. They are pure math: they do not talk to motors or sensors, so they work the same in the simulator as on a robot.

4. Measure from the robot to a point

Where this goes: replace only the robotInit() method from your earlier lessons, from its @Override line through that method’s matching closing brace. Keep the rest of the file. Do not add a second robotInit().

Code to use

@Override
public void robotInit() {
    Pose2d robot = new Pose2d(2.0, 1.5, Rotation2d.fromDegrees(90));
    Translation2d target = new Translation2d(4.6, 4.0);

    Translation2d toTarget = target.minus(robot.getTranslation());
    double distance = toTarget.getNorm();
    Rotation2d direction = toTarget.getAngle();
    Rotation2d turn = direction.minus(robot.getRotation());

    System.out.println("Robot at x=" + robot.getX() + " y=" + robot.getY());
    System.out.println("Distance to target: " + String.format("%.2f", distance) + " m");
    System.out.println("Field direction to target: " + String.format("%.1f", direction.getDegrees()) + " degrees");
    System.out.println("Turn needed: " + String.format("%.1f", turn.getDegrees()) + " degrees");
}
PieceWhat it does here
new Pose2d(2.0, 1.5, Rotation2d.fromDegrees(90))A robot 2.0 m from the blue wall, 1.5 m from the side wall, facing +Y.
target.minus(robot.getTranslation())The arrow from the robot to the target: how far to go in x (2.6 m) and in y (2.5 m).
toTarget.getNorm()The length of that arrow. It is the Pythagorean theorem: √(2.6² + 2.5²).
toTarget.getAngle()The direction of that arrow on the field map, as a Rotation2d.
direction.minus(robot.getRotation())How far the robot must turn from the way it faces now.
String.format("%.2f", distance)Turns the number into text with 2 digits after the decimal point, so the output is easy to read.

5. Read the answer

The target is 3.61 m away. On the field map, the direction from the robot to the target is 43.9°. The robot faces 90°. Subtract: 43.9° − 90° = −46.1°.

A negative turn means clockwise when you look down from above. For a robot, that is a turn to its right.

This one subtraction is the core of aiming. Robot code that keeps the robot facing a point does it about 50 times a second: find the direction, subtract the heading, turn by the difference.

6. Same map, either alliance

When your team is on the red alliance, the things you care about are often on the other side of the field. The points change: a target that sits near the blue wall for blue robots sits near the red wall for red robots. The map does not change, and the heading math in section 4 does not change.

A 2026 lessonNever add 180° to a heading because “we’re red.” In 2026, aiming was 180° off on the red alliance because of exactly that kind of shortcut. The robot code now mirrors target points in one place, keeps all heading math on the blue-corner map, and lets the swerve library handle the driver’s point of view.

Challenge: which heading needs no turn?

Compare it with your code. target.minus(robot.getTranslation()) is the arrow from the robot to the target, getNorm() its length, and this logged heading error is the robot’s heading minus the target heading. Its sign is the opposite of our lesson’s “turn needed”; the controller uses the target heading, while an aimed check can use the error’s absolute value. AllianceUtil.flipIfRed mirrors the target for red: the target is written once, in blue coordinates. .ifPresent(...) is the 2027 version of getAngle() saying “there may be no angle”.

2027 differencesThe practice bot runs an early (alpha) version of WPILib 2027. There, these classes live in org.wpilib.math.geometry instead of edu.wpi.first.math.geometry, Rotation2d.kZero becomes Rotation2d.ZERO, and Translation2d.getAngle() can say “no angle” for an arrow of length zero. WPILib has also said the 2027 field map might use a different origin. The idea of a pose does not change. Keep your own practice on WPILib 2026.
