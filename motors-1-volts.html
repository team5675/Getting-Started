Volts make a motor move

What a motor controller does with a volts request, how fast a motor spins for each volt, and why it never jumps to full speed.

01 See it Follow the idea02 Try it Predict with numbers03 Check it Explain what happened

Today’s small goal

Explain what a robot program’s volts request does to a motor, predict a roller’s speed from its volts, and name the two numbers that describe a motor: kS and kV. Motor lab 1 measures them on a simulated roller.

Before you start: finish Lab 7. This page is reading and arithmetic only; nothing to run yet. Allow 40–55 minutes.

Part 4 uses invented teaching numbers and a generic roller. It is not a 2027 game mechanism.

Optional shortcut · already know motors?

A motor holds 10 rps at 2.5 V and 40 rps at 8.5 V. What are its kS and kV, and what volts would hold 25 rps?

Compare your answer

kV = (8.5 − 2.5) ÷ (40 − 10) = 0.2 V per rps. kS = 2.5 − 0.2 × 10 = 0.5 V. For 25 rps: 0.5 + 0.2 × 25 = 5.5 V.

If that was easy, skim to Try it. Otherwise read the whole page.

1. See it: the program asks for volts

Lab 7’s pretend roller asked for an output of 0.4, and nothing moved, because no motor was connected. On a real robot, 0.4 sent to a motor controller means “40 percent of the battery”: about 4.8 V from a 12 V battery.

Team code usually asks for volts instead. A battery sags during a match, from about 12.5 V down to 11 V or less. Forty percent of a sagging battery is fewer volts each minute, but a request for 4.8 V stays 4.8 V. So the same code behaves the same way all match.

The motor controller is the small computer that switches battery power on to the motor. On the team’s robots it is a TalonFX, built into each Kraken X60 motor. Your program never touches the battery; it sends the controller a request, and the controller does the switching.

The controller only obeys while the robot is enabled. When the robot is disabled, every motor controller sends 0 V, whatever the program asks. That is a safety rule built into FRC hardware.

2. See it: rotations per second, free speed and gearing

Team code measures speed in rotations per second, written rps. Motor data sheets often say rpm (rotations per minute). Divide rpm by 60 to get rps.

FactNumberWhy it matters
Kraken X60 free speed at 12 Vabout 6000 rpm = 100 rps“Free” means nothing attached: the fastest it can spin.
A 3:1 gear reductionmotor turns 3 times per roller turnThe roller turns 3 times slower, with 3 times the twist (torque).
Roller free speed at 12 V100 ÷ 3 ≈ 33 rpsThe most speed the roller can ever have.

Here is the key idea: steady speed grows in step with volts. Half the volts gives about half the speed.

PAUSE & PREDICT

Half the battery

The roller above gets 6 V, half of 12 V. Predict its steady speed in rps before you read on.

Half of 33 rps is about 16.5 rps, and that is a good prediction. Motor lab 1 measures the real answer in the simulation. It comes out a little lower. Something uses up a few volts before the roller can turn at all: friction.

3. See it: two numbers describe a motor

Picture a fictional practice wheel (not the lab’s roller). You give it steady volts, wait until the speed stops changing, and write down the speed. Two readings: 2.5 V gives 10 rps, and 8.5 V gives 40 rps. Put them on a graph and join them with a straight line.

The line tells you two numbers, and every team motor configuration uses them:

  • kS, the “static” volts: what friction uses up before anything moves. Here kS = 0.5 V. Below 0.5 V, the wheel does not turn at all.
  • kV, the “velocity” volts: the extra volts for each extra 1 rps. Here kV = 0.2 V per rps. It is the slope of the line: rise ÷ run.

Together they make one sentence you can calculate with:

volts = kS + kV × speed

For example, to hold the practice wheel at 25 rps: 0.5 + 0.2 × 25 = 0.5 + 5 = 5.5 V. Turn it around to predict a speed from volts: speed = (volts − kS) ÷ kV. At 6.5 V: (6.5 − 0.5) ÷ 0.2 = 30 rps.

Why “static” and “velocity”? S is for static friction, which holds a thing still until you push hard enough. V is for velocity, which is speed. You will meet the same letters in Motor lab 3 in the TalonFX’s settings, with exactly the same meaning.

4. See it: speeding up takes time

A motor never jumps to its steady speed. When the volts first arrive, the motor is not turning yet, so nothing pushes back. The current is enormous: a stopped Kraken X60 given 6 V would draw about 180 amps. That current makes a big twist, so the wheel speeds up quickly. As it speeds up, the motor pushes back harder, the current falls, and the speeding-up slows down.

Two facts from this picture matter on every robot:

  • Logs need time to settle. To read a steady speed, wait until the line is flat. Reading too early gives a speed that is too low.
  • Starting is the hard part. The starting surge is why teams set current limits. Many motors surging at once can pull the battery voltage down so far that the robot controller switches its outputs off to protect itself: a brownout. Motor lab 3 sets a current limit.

5. See it: never ask for more than the battery has

A 12 V battery cannot give a motor 20 V. If code asks for more, the controller gives what it can. Good code keeps its own requests in range, so the logged request matches what really happened. WPILib’s MathUtil.clamp does that in one line: it returns the value, or the nearest limit if the value is outside.

CallReturns
MathUtil.clamp(5.0, -12.0, 12.0)5.0: already inside the limits
MathUtil.clamp(20.0, -12.0, 12.0)12.0: the top limit
MathUtil.clamp(-20.0, -12.0, 12.0)-12.0: the bottom limit, for full speed backwards

6. Try it: predict with kS and kV

Use the practice wheel: kS = 0.5 V and kV = 0.2 V per rps. Work each one out on paper before opening the answer.

  1. What volts hold the wheel at 45 rps?
    Check

    0.5 + 0.2 × 45 = 0.5 + 9 = 9.5 V.

  2. What steady speed do you get at 4.5 V?
    Check

    (4.5 − 0.5) ÷ 0.2 = 4 ÷ 0.2 = 20 rps.

  3. What happens at 0.3 V?
    Check

    Nothing moves. 0.3 V is less than kS = 0.5 V, so friction wins.

  4. A teammate says “double the volts, double the speed”. Test it with 2.5 V and 5 V. Is it exactly true?
    Check

    2.5 V gives 10 rps; 5 V gives (5 − 0.5) ÷ 0.2 = 22.5 rps, a bit more than double. The kS volts are “spent” on friction both times, so doubling the volts more than doubles the part that makes speed. It is close, not exact.

  5. Code asks for MathUtil.clamp(15.0, -12.0, 12.0) volts. What speed does the wheel settle at?
    Check

    The request becomes 12 V: (12 − 0.5) ÷ 0.2 = 57.5 rps.

Before you move on

In your own words: what do kS and kV mean, and why does a stopped motor draw the most current? Write one prediction for Motor lab 1 in learning-notes.txt: “At 6 V the lab roller will settle at about ___ rps, a little less than half of 33 because ___.”

Connection to team code

Every team motor configuration has a place for kS and kV, and the drivetrain’s settings from the CTRE swerve generator have them too. Teams measure them with WPILib’s SysId tool, which runs the same kinds of tests as Motor lab 1: a slow ramp of volts, and a sudden step of volts. You will do both by hand first, so the numbers SysId prints will make sense.
